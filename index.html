Context / introduction

For some context, congestion prices in downtown Manhattan were there because it was obviously a high-traffic area, and trains would logically cut car use by discouraging people who drive from taking a car and getting them onto the MTA instead, but then the issue was whether this fee was eliminating the pollution by actually encouraging more MTA use, or whether it was increasing car use on other pathways, so we looked north of 60th Street, where the fee never started, and the reason we picked the Bronx, and the reason we had to use bridges, was that the paid zone stopped at 60th Street and there were no fee gates north of it, so the only vehicle counts that existed up there were on the bridges, and the Bronx crossings were the only place we could ask whether cars spilled, and the gate file could not see Mott Haven at all because no gate stood there.

The fee file

A car paid $9 in the morning and $2.25 at night, so more trucks went at night, because if you're a company you'd cut your trucking cost by running overnight, and that was exactly what the 2025 fee file showed, but we could not say the trucks "moved" to the night, though, because the file had no before to compare against.

We used this fee file only to describe the gates and the night trucks, and we picked Bridges and Tunnels for the spill test because that one had a before.

Did cars spill?

We compared Bronx bridges to far bridges outside the fee, this year against last year. If cars were dodging downtown through the Bronx, the Bronx line should have climbed after January 2025. Over 2025 the Bronx grew about 1.04 percentage points slower than the far bridges, with an interval from -3.6 to +1.6 and a p-value of about 0.49. That interval crossed zero and the p-value was high, so we had no statistically significant evidence of a spill.

We ran the same check on the tunnels that go into the paid zone. Those tunnels came in 2.29 points below the far bridges, with an interval from -3.4 to -1.2. That interval stayed below zero, so the fee looked like it was doing its job. January was the biggest drop, at -9.18 points.

We got the same null when we shuffled the labels. Cars did not spill into the Bronx in a detectable way. Counts came from MTA Bridges and Tunnels.

The air

The dirt everyone was measuring was PM2.5, and it came off tailpipes, brakes, and tires, and the dots on the map were every monitor result we could find, colored by whether the air went down, went up, or did not move.

Fraser's paper, Figure 1, showed the paid zone got about 22 percent cleaner than a no-fee model of the same six months, about 3 µg/m³, and that drop shrank as you left the zone, and Barber's thesis, Table A.8, showed downtown falling from 8.70 to 7.56, which was 13 percent, and he quoted Fraser's 22 percent on page 25 but did not claim it as his own, and South Bronx Unite put up 19 community sensors, and twelve to fourteen went up, and the biggest jump was +1.29 µg/m³ at the Deegan by the Third Avenue Bridge, right next to East 138th, and the city health department ran official monitors for a year with the Van Wyck as a control, and they found no fee-sized change they could call, and they noted traffic was only about 14 percent of PM2.5 here, so the honest answer was three answers, and we did not average them, because inside the zone the air got cleaner, and outside it depended on the monitor, and in Mott Haven the nearest official monitor was on East 135th, and it was never one of Fraser's six, while the community sensors said the Deegan got a little worse, and that was why the trees had to answer to the Deegan, not to the fee.

Where is the one specific location

Picking one place wasn't that hard once we looked at the map, and we knew it had to be the Bronx, because congestion paths climbed from the bottom of Manhattan up, and the analysis top options were all Bronx luckily, and then highest priority was closest to the congestion zone AND aligning with the other MTA / air data we already had, and that landed on the Deegan at East 138th, and other hot areas were harder because trees were already there, so it was big risk big reward and needed something really innovative, and money was already going toward Deegan road work luckily, which helped the case, and East 138th was where the Third Avenue Bridge landed in the Bronx, and it was also where the Major Deegan exit dumped into the neighborhood, so it took bridge traffic and highway traffic at the same time, and the one community sensor that got worse after the fee was right at the Deegan by that bridge (+1.29 µg/m³), and the city's own Mott Haven monitor sat on East 135th, about 330 meters south, and the street ran 1.6 km through Hunts Point, Longwood, and Melrose, about 40 meters wide face to face, median H/W 0.38, so nine of ten blocks were open enough for trees to actually work, and adults here went to the ER for asthma at 193.5 per 10,000 against 66.4 citywide, and heat score was 5 out of 5, and poverty was 29.3 percent, so those neighborhood numbers said this street deserved the work first, but they couldn't pick one block over another, and that was what the block table was for.

What we can buy now

We didn't invent a new budget. Parks and DEP already had money in them, so we could start planting Mott Haven streets now.

Open trees and rain gardens on the open blocks, and the map on the right shows where.

We skipped dense leafy roofs near the highway. Those trap air at breathing height instead of clearing it.

i-Tree said the corridor program removed about 10.6 pounds of PM2.5 a year and caught about 7.2 million gallons of stormwater. That does not cancel a +1.29 µg/m³ highway hit. So the lid stays for later.

We weren't architects. The Deegan sits in a trench, exhaust at breathing height, and trees only help around the edges. The bigger move was a lid over that sunken road: cars under, park on top, vents for the fumes (Klyde Warren in Dallas already did this). About East 138th to East 149th, ~12.9 acres, roughly $250M to $1.2B, and those were estimates. Deegan is a state road, so the clock is years. We plant now and put the lid on the table.
