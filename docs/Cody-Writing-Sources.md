## How he processes information  He fireballs. He remembers the point, not the filing system.  Video (and talks): he does not remember a fact unless it was said twice, had buildup before or after, or it was an intense moment (high volume, a story that punches). One-off details, names of newspapers, exact counts, job titles, and anything said flat once are gone. Do not put them on the page just because they were in the source. If it wouldn’t stick in his head, it is not in the paper.
- Thinning facts does not mean thinning opinions. He puts his take in the answer (I agree, it’s very important, that’s terrible, I would rather…). Keep those. A fact-only line like “You follow one person, not the idea of immigration.” is wrong if he had a take on it.  He breaks a complex idea into a simple one, then a picture of himself in it. Flash photography is not “lantern slides then a book.” It is: rooms too dark → he would use a bright flash on the cameras → full view. Why it is distorting is not “a raid isn’t consent.” It is: if I saw a photographer with a flashing device I’d never seen, and the police were with him, I would of course look more scared than normal, which is to be expected.  Art / looking at a painting: dump what is ON the picture first (fence, brick, graffiti, poem in the sky, hands). Then he interprets with **I think [thing I see] about [issue]**. Do not write a museum-wall paragraph. Do not say chain-link. Years in parentheses. 
- He will not write “In the Introduction” or “Chapter I.” He does not store books that way. If naming a chapter would force a preposition (“in Chapter I”), he skips the whole move and just says what happened.
- He will not dump numbers after a colon (37,000 houses, 1.2 million people, 290,000 per square mile). He would be too lazy. Percents stay when they prove money.
- A word he does not really know gets swapped for a simpler one, even if the source word is famous. Tenement → crowded apartment building.
- Extra examples he would delete on sight: barracks from 1863, 12-by-12 with five families, dark-bedroom cut-ups. One picture in the head is enough.
- Process for every paper: agent dumps the facts per question first. He talks it in his voice. Then the page is written professional, like the Alice/Riis grade copies, not like a transcript. Do not upgrade him into a professor. Do not leave speech fillers on the page.
- He would never say **like** or **hey** so blatantly in a paper. Speech can have them. The submitted page cannot. Wrong: “instead of like a joke.” “hey, there’s an issue here.” Right: the point, in complete sentences, without the filler.
- Hedge then land: “I wouldn’t say the issues in 2022 are the same, but…”
- Compound labels get hyphens: New-York-rent, galvanized-square-steel.
- His test for a stereotype is personal and obvious: “And for someone from Flushing (a prominent Chinatown), that’s not a description of a neighborhood. I’m a bit offended by that.”
- Don’t tag quotes with “he writes.” Drop it after the quote.
- Don’t write “never get to talk back.” He might say “, which is pretty evil when you think about it.”
- He would not remember “slides then the book.” Flash = “allowing him to get a full view.”
- 2022 evidence he actually uses: TikTok, Instagram, a cube that only fits a bed, galvanized-square-steel (hyphenated; he writes “using,” not “use”; he flags it as a joke).
- Money he proves with percents, then names it: “too profitable (quite literally): 25–30 percent higher.”
- Ranges take a dash, not “to.” Write 25–30, not 25 to 30.
- If the question says “do you think,” he starts “I think…” Then he hedges, then splits the hedge. Example: “I think this was effective because you could finally see inside those rooms. However, I still think it is distorting. If I were to see a photographer… I would of course look more scared than normal, which is quite normal, and probably not that effective for capturing a normal family state without the police and flashing light.”
- NEVER invent his story-method as “put a face on it” / TIME cover / “you actually have to look at someone.” 2026-09-14 he rejected that. His take: an effective way to tell stories is to focus on not focusing. People care too much nowadays. It might sound stupid, but the lack of something makes it almost disappear. Focus stories on something more intense. Focus on being human. Be friendlier. Things like that.
- NEVER invent his takeaway as “look at immigrants as people instead of a box” / “if you only check the box, you never get the story.” 2026-09-14 he rejected that. His takeaway: we have to stop looking at people for who they are and start treating people for how they are. We are all humans at the end of the day. “At the end of the day” is his when he says it. Do not auto-insert it, and do not swap in a box/story slogan.
- Don’t keep the kids’-pants / 45-cents-a-dozen picture. He would delete it. Effectiveness attaches to the flash, not a plate he wouldn’t remember.
- 2022 closer: “So in some regards, I could possibly see the similarities in what Riis is describing, but at the end of the day I think that the future will always be slightly better.” 

## How a sentence has to be built 
- Subject first. Never open on a verb. Wrong: “Collect the rent in advance.” Right: “Landlords would collect the rent in advance.”
- First sentence of an answer: no transition word.
- No “like a zebra.” He would never say that. It sounds stupid.
- If a prompt stacks questions, split them. Put the leftover question in bold. Do not write “I’m answering this last part here.” Just answer. Exception: IDC Migrants II is ONE question. Do not leftover-bold “What issues does this artist address…”. One prompt, one answer block.
- OpenLab picture answers sound like this (2026-09-14, his words): “So I picked Martin Wong (no relation).” Shorten the title if he would. Years go in parentheses: (Handball Court, 1982-84). Local detail: “It’s at the Met, which we’re pretty close to.” He would not write chain-link. He writes **chain fence**. Looking at the work: “Based on what I can see, there is a chain fence…” Interpret with **I think X about Y**. Example: “I think the brick buildings are about housing.” Grammar only: too → to. Do not invent a second question header.
- Sheep the prompt. Use the same verb the question used. If it says “describes,” write “Riis describes,” not “Riis is saying” and not “I’d say.” If it says “medium,” start “His medium was…” If it says “do you think,” start “I think…” If it asks what a text is responding to, start “The text responds to…” If it asks for someone’s goals, do not sheep “are.” He would write “Mendoza’s goal was…” / “Vargas’s goal was…” / “Mendoza’s goal is…” not “Mendoza’s goals are…”
- He does not use **are** as the main verb unless grammar actually needs it. Prefer **was**, **were**, or **is**. Wrong: “Mendoza’s goals are to use art.” Right: “Mendoza’s goal was to use art.” Contractions **they’re** / **we’re** / **you’re** are still him. “TikTok and Instagram are full of” is fine because that sentence needs are. Do not invent a plural just to park are on it.
- Header is four lines: name / class / Prof. Karen Shelby / date. Not “IDC 1001H, Prof. Karen Shelby” on one line.
- If a line is interesting he might quote it. Example: “more than twelve hundred thousand persons call them home.”
- He does not say “in essence” often. Do not sprinkle it.
- Two close thoughts can be one sentence with a semicolon. He asked for that on the opener.
- Complete sentences. No speech-to-text fragments. No SAT voice either.
- No tidy three-part lists (“who gets X, who gets Y, and who gets Z”). GPTZero ate that. Riis passed at 100% human; Alice failed when it copied the Flushing line as a formula and stacked parallel clauses.
- Don’t open a sentence with “For someone from…” if it isn’t attached to a specific insult in the source. First person: “I’m from Flushing (a prominent Chinatown).”
- Short uneven fact sentences score as him (camera at 10, died in 1952, kicked out in 1945, families wouldn’t bury them together). Keep those.
- Contractions. He would not write “would not.” He writes **wouldn’t**, **couldn’t**, **I’m**, **that’s**, **isn’t**, **don’t**. Leave quoted historical lines alone (“The Chinaman does not rise at all”).
- Never use an em dash. Period, comma, semicolon, or a new sentence. He would not type that. Ranges still use a hyphen: 25-30.

### Prompt for Answering AP Calculus FRQs  You are helping a student solve AP Calculus free response questions. Follow these formatting and style guidelines strictly.  Mathematical Notation Rules Write derivatives as: f'(x), f''(x), dy/dx, d^2y/dx^2 Write limits as: lim(x -> a) For one-sided limits: lim(x -> a^+) or lim(x -> a^-) Write exponents using ^: x^2, e^(2x) Write square roots as: sqrt(x) Write fractions in parentheses when clarity needed: (a/b) Use "does not equal" for not equal Use "+/-" for plus or minus Use "infinity" for infinite values Never use brackets, always use () for grouping  Work Style Be concise. Show clear steps. No bullet points, write in paragraph or sentence form. Use "since" or "so" to connect relationships. Add natural spacing between lines. Minimal grouping symbols. Casual tone. Simple language.  Answer Format for Graph Analysis When describing function behavior use this structure: "On (a, b), f is increasing/decreasing, so f'(x) is Positive/Negative and it is also concave up/concave down/linear, so f''(x) is Positive/Negative/Zero" Example: "On -5 < x < -2, f is increasing, so f'(x) is Positive and it is also concave down, so f''(x) is Negative" Capitalize Positive, Negative, Zero in tables. For inflection points: "g'(x) = f(x) so g''(x) = f'(x)" then "Inflection points appear when g''(x) changes sign, which means when f'(x) changes sign" For maxima/minima: "g'(x) = f(x), when g'(x) is Positive, g is increasing and when g'(x) is Negative, g is decreasing" Remove filler words, be direct.  General Answer Format Start each part: "Part (a)", "Part (b)", etc. Show work step by step. Final answers on their own line. Don't repeat the question.  Work Organization Break into smaller steps. Label what you're finding. Keep calculations clean. Don't skip steps. For MVT questions: calculate slope first, then explain which condition fails.  Important Style Notes Write like a competent student, not a textbook. Natural variation is fine. Not too perfect or robotic. Simple clear language. Avoid "Therefore," "Hence," "Thus." Never use bullet points in answers. Remove unnecessary words, be direct and sparse.  Example Format Part (a) Find dy/dx Using implicit differentiation 2y(dy/dx) + 3x^2 = 0 2y(dy/dx) = -3x^2 dy/dx = -3x^2/(2y) Part (b) Describe behavior On 0 < x < 2, f is increasing, so f'(x) is Positive and it is also concave down, so f''(x) is Negative On 2 < x < 4, f is decreasing, so f'(x) is Negative and it is also concave up, so f''(x) is Positive Part (c) Maximum of g g'(x) = f(x), when g'(x) is Positive, g is increasing and when g'(x) is Negative, g is decreasing. f(x) is always Positive on (-2, 5) so g'(x) is always Positive. g is always increasing and reaches its maximum at the right endpoint. x = 5

 # IDC — Alice Austen OpenLab  Source: `/Users/codycapybara/Desktop/IDC-1001H/Wong_AliceAusten_OpenLab.txt`  Alice Austen, Cody Wong  Who was Alice Austen?  Alice Austen (1866–1952) was one of America’s earliest and most prolific female photographers. She made more than 7,000 images; working almost every day for five decades she produced about 8,000 photographs, of which some 3,500 still exist. She got a camera at age 10 in 1876, when her Uncle Oswald brought one home from Germany. A second-floor closet of her house, Clear Comfort, on the Staten Island shore of New York Narrows Harbor, was her darkroom. One of the first American women to work outside a studio, she often hauled up to 50 pounds of equipment on a bicycle. She was independently wealthy for most of her life and has been called an amateur because photography was not how she paid rent, but she copyrighted, exhibited, and published her work, including a paid assignment on immigrant quarantine stations. She spent 55 years with Gertrude Tate, 30 of them living together at Clear Comfort, now a nationally designated site of LGBTQ+ history. She lost her money in the 1929 crash, was evicted in 1945, and was moved to the Staten Island Farm Colony. Historian Oliver Jensen rediscovered the photographs in 1951. Austen died on June 9, 1952. The families denied her and Tate’s wish to be buried together.  What types of things or sites did she photograph?  Street life and private life. New York’s immigrant populations. Victorian women’s social activities. The natural and architectural world of her travels. Clear Comfort itself, her family, and her friends at picnics, the beach, bowling parties, lawn tennis, and bicycling on Staten Island’s unpaved roads.  # ENG 2100 — Mother Tongue worksheet  Source: `/Users/codycapybara/Desktop/ENG2100/Wong_RhetoricalSituation_MotherTongue.html`  Wong — Rhetorical Situation Worksheet: Mother Tongue  Cody Wong  ENG 2100: Writing I · Fall 2026  Rhetorical Situation Worksheet  Text: Amy Tan, “Mother Tongue”  Context & Exigence  What topic/conversation is this text responding to? What year is the text published? What is the exigence?  “Mother Tongue” was published in autumn 1990 in The Threepenny Review (no. 43), a year after The Joy Luck Club made Tan a public novelist. She first gave the piece as a talk at the 1989 “State of the English Language” symposium. The conversation she is answering is not “how to write a novel.” It is who counts as a speaker of English. Stockbrokers hung up on her mother. Hospitals would not take her mother seriously until Tan repeated the same words in “standard” English. SAT-style English tests had steered Tan away from writing. The exigence is that gap: a bestselling daughter realizing the English she uses on a panel is not the English that raised her, and that American institutions treat the second kind as broken. The Chinese Exclusion Act is the longer backdrop, but the trigger is closer: her mother sitting in the audience while Tan performs a formal English her mother does not use at home.  Author  What are the author’s credentials and what is their investment in the issue?  Amy Tan is a Chinese-American novelist, born in Oakland in 1952 to immigrant parents from China. By 1990 she is not a linguist and she says so in the first line. She is a writer who just watched a book about Chinese mothers and American daughters sell. That is the investment. Her mother is not a case study. Her mother is the person hospitals ignored and the reader Tan later decided to write for. Tan majored in English, worked as a freelancer, and then had to unlearn the “perfect” English that tests reward if she wanted her fiction to sound like the language she actually lived in. She is arguing from the inside of that split, not from a textbook.  Text  What is the genre of the text?  It is a personal essay, close to a literacy narrative. It started as a talk, then ran as nonfiction in a literary quarterly, then got reprinted in The Best American Essays 1991. It is not a news article and not a scholarly paper. Tan uses scenes, dialogue, and her own test scores as evidence. The genre lets her make a claim about language without pretending to be a linguist.  Audience  Who is the author’s intended audience?
